The Hangar Rent Math: What the Lease Income Covers — and What It Doesn't

A T-hangar rents for $250 to $850 a month. A corporate box rents for $1,500 to $5,000. The gap between those two numbers is the whole investment case — and the reason most hangars do not pay for themselves.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The numbers were on a whiteboard in the airport manager's office, and they did not add up the way the owner hoped. A 50-by-50 corporate box hangar at Thermal, California — Jacqueline Cochran Regional Airport — rents for $1,935 a month on a one-year lease, or $2,940 month to month. The hangar cost $270,000 to build. The ground lease ran $0.36 a square foot. The loan, at 9.75 percent over ten years, cost about $3,500 a month. The rent covered barely half the payment. The owner stared at the whiteboard for a while, then asked the question every hangar investor eventually asks: what does the rent actually cover? The answer is less than the brochure promises, and more than the skeptics claim — and the difference between the two is the airport, the lease term, and the size of the box.

Start with what tenants actually pay. There is no national database of hangar rents, but the published 2026 rates tell a consistent story. A typical T-hangar for a single-engine aircraft runs $250 to $850 a month, with older simple T-hangars at $100 to $300 and modern ones at busy metropolitan airports reaching $500 to $1,000 or more. The regional spread is wide: $200 to $400 a month in the Deep South and the Midwest, $300 to $600 in Texas and the Mountain West, $400 to $1,200 in Florida, and $350 to $1,500 or more on the West Coast and in the Northeast. Published airport rates make it concrete. Minot, North Dakota, charges about $130 a month for a T-hangar. Boulder, Colorado, charges $350 to $550. Livermore, California, charges $463 to $616. Paine Field in Washington charges $554 to $859. Arlington Municipal in Texas manages more than 100 T-hangars at $270 to $425 a month, with box hangars at $425 to $500. Tacoma Narrows in Washington charges $437.85 to $501.64 a month for a T-hangar in 2026, and $2,157.75 for a 65-by-55 corporate hangar — $2,797.20 with an office and bathroom. Santa Monica's T-hangar waitlist runs 24 months. Two fields 20 miles apart can differ by half.

Box hangars run roughly two to three times the T-hangar figure, and corporate hangars run further still. At Thermal, the ladder is precise: a 42-by-32 T-hangar rents for $745 a month on a one-year lease; a 46-by-40 T-hangar for $895; a 46-by-45 business box for $1,415; a 55-by-40 corporate box for $1,505; a 46-by-55 corporate box for $1,725; and a 50-by-50 corporate box for $1,935. The month-to-month rates run about 50 percent higher across the board — $1,130 for the smallest T-hangar, $2,940 for the corporate box. Executive and corporate hangars with offices, restrooms, heating, and upgraded electrical run $1,500 to $5,000 or more a month, with large premium-market facilities costing considerably more. Transient rates tell the other side of the market: a single-engine airplane pays $75 a night at Thermal, or $1,350 a month; a very light jet pays $125 a night, or $2,250 a month; a light jet pays $200 a night, or $3,595 a month. The transient market is real income, but it is lumpy — it arrives with the weather and the airshow calendar, and it leaves the same way.

Run the debt service against those rents and the investment case sharpens. A $270,000 hangar financed at 60 percent loan-to-value — $162,000 borrowed — at 9.75 percent over ten years costs about $2,090 a month in debt service. A corporate box renting at $1,935 a month does not cover it. The same hangar at the 20-year Georgia public rate of 4.03 percent costs about $990 a month, and the rent covers it twice over — but that rate is for public airports, not private borrowers. The T-hangar math is worse: a $120,000 T-hangar build financed at $72,000 costs about $930 a month at 9.75 percent over ten years, against rent of $250 to $850. The gap is the owner's equity, and it is large: at 60 percent LTV, the owner puts $48,000 down on the T-hangar and $108,000 down on the corporate box, then covers a monthly shortfall on top. Insurance, maintenance, and the ground lease add more — annual upkeep runs $5,000 to $15,000, and the ground lease at $0.36 a square foot costs $900 a year on a 2,500-square-foot box, before the CPI escalator. The rent covers the operating costs. It rarely covers the debt.

That is the honest answer, and it is why the hangar-as-investment case usually rests on something other than rent. The first something is appreciation: at a constrained field with a 24-month waitlist, a hangar on a long ground lease is an appreciating asset, and owners who bought structures for $60,000 to $400,000 have watched them rise with the waitlist. The second is the buy-versus-rent math for the owner's own airplane: at $600 or more a month in rent, a purchased hangar on a long ground lease frequently wins over a decade of ownership, and the owner is paying himself instead of a landlord. The third is the multi-year lease discount: a three-year commitment often yields 10 to 15 percent off the monthly rate, and a five-year deal can reach 20 percent — which matters to the tenant, and to the owner who wants a stable tenant rather than a higher headline rent. The fourth is the tax treatment, which varies by structure and is the one part of the analysis that belongs to the owner's accountant, not to a rent survey.

The counterargument is that the rent math is the wrong test, and the hangar owners who say so have a point. A hangar is not an apartment building. Nobody buys a hangar for the yield; they buy it because they own an airplane that needs a roof, or because they run a business that needs one, and the rent is a way to defray the cost of an asset they would own anyway. The investor who underwrites a hangar like a multifamily property — demanding a 1.25x debt service coverage ratio from rent alone — will conclude that no hangar pencils, and will be right, and will miss the point. The hangar's return is the airplane it protects, the business it houses, and the option value of a scarce slot at a constrained airport. The rent is the subsidy, not the return. Underwrite the subsidy honestly and the asset makes sense. Underwrite it as the return and it never does.

What an owner should do is run the numbers in the right order. Price the hangar at the specific airport, not the regional average, because two fields 20 miles apart can differ by half. Get the ground lease term in writing and check the reversion clause — when the lease expires, ownership of the structure often reverts to the airport, which caps the appreciation story at the lease term. Model the debt service at 60 percent LTV and the actual rate the borrower can get, not the public-program rate the borrower cannot. Then ask whether the rent, the appreciation, and the avoided rent on the owner's own airplane together cover the payment. If they do, the hangar is a good asset. If only the rent covers it, the hangar is a bad investment wearing a good story. And if the waitlist is 24 months and the ground lease has 40 years left, the story may be better than the rent suggests — which is exactly why the waitlist, not the rent roll, is the number to check first.
