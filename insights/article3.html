Buy the Roof or Build It

A hangar is the cheapest part of owning a jet and the hardest part to finance. The arithmetic decides which side of that line an owner stands on.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 8, 2026 · 7 min read

The owner had two numbers on the table. One was a 12,000-square-foot hangar for sale at his home field, built twenty years ago, with a door that worked and a roof that did not leak. The other was a quote to build the same footprint new, on a ground lease the airport would sign for forty years. The existing hangar cost less than the new one. The new one cost less than he expected. And the difference between the two — the part nobody had put in a spreadsheet — was the loan. The bank would lend on the existing building. It would not lend on the one that did not exist yet, on land the borrower did not own, to a borrower whose collateral was a jet the bank had never financed.

Start with the building, because the building is the smaller number. A turnkey hangar — structure, doors, electrical, lighting, insulation, finished interior — runs $65 to $150 per square foot in 2026, according to the CNSS cost guide, while the steel shell alone runs $25 to $45. A second guide puts the working range at $60 to $120 per square foot, which makes a 15,000-square-foot hangar $900,000 to $1,800,000 before land and site work. The spread is not noise. It is the difference between a T-hangar and a box hangar, between a basic shell and a finished facility with heat, air, and a door wide enough for a jet: Dataintelo's private hangar construction report puts T-hangars at $42 to $68 per square foot and box hangars at $58 to $94, and a Texas engineering guide puts basic construction at roughly $50 to $150 per square foot, with Florida running higher for wind-rated doors and structure. Site work — grading, drainage, utility extension — is a separate cost in every guide, and on an undeveloped parcel it can add a meaningful share of the total.

Now the loan. AOPA Finance offers aircraft loan terms of 5 to 20 years with a typical down payment of 15%, and its published rates for experimental aircraft run 4.99% to 6.9%, with piston-aircraft rates at 6.99% to 9.99% as of November 2026. Those are aircraft rates, and a hangar loan is not an aircraft loan — but the terms are the nearest honest benchmark an owner has, because the hangar lender, when one can be found, prices off the same borrower and the same credit. Take a $1,200,000 build: 15% down is $180,000, leaving a $1,020,000 loan. At 7% over 20 years, the payment is about $7,908 a month, and the total interest over the life of the loan is about $877,932 — nearly three-quarters of the amount borrowed, paid again. At 6%, the same loan costs about $7,308 a month. One point of rate, on a twenty-year hangar loan, is worth about $600 a month and about $145,000 over the life of the deal. That is the price of finding the right lender instead of the first one.

The state programs exist because that price is often out of reach. Nebraska's Revolving Hangar Loan Program lends up to 80% of eligible costs to a maximum of $1,000,000 per project, with a 20-year repayment period and no interest at all — a fund established in 1992 and re-approved in May 2026. On a $1,200,000 build, that program covers $960,000 at 0%, which is a $4,000-a-month payment with no interest at all, against $7,908 a month at 7% from a private lender. The catch is eligibility: the borrower is a municipality operating a public-use airport, not a private owner, and the loan is for the airport's hangar, not the owner's. California's program is the same shape — state loans to public airports, maximum term 17 years. The private owner who wants to build his own hangar cannot use either program. He is back in the private market, where the lender has to understand ground leases and the borrower has to find that lender.

Buying instead of building changes the arithmetic in one way and not in another. An existing hangar is a building a bank can see, appraise, and take a lien on — which is why the bank in the opening story would lend on it and not on the new one. But the existing hangar is also twenty years old, its door is twenty years old, its insulation is twenty years old, and its price reflects a market where every hangar at the airport is full and the waiting list is real. In Fayetteville, Arkansas, in 2006, the city built two corporate hangars with state grants and a $700,000 city loan because every hangar was full and private investors would not build on leased land — and the same shortage that made the city build is what makes an existing hangar expensive to buy. The buyer pays for scarcity. The builder pays for risk. There is no third option.

The counterargument is that the cheapest roof is the one someone else owns. Renting hangar space avoids the down payment, the construction risk, and the twenty-year commitment, and for an owner who flies 150 hours a year, the rent is a rounding error against the cost of the jet. That is true, and it is also the argument that stops working the day the FBO has no space to rent. The FAA counted 213,756 active general-aviation and Part 135 aircraft in the United States in 2024, flying more than 29 million hours, and general aviation contributed $339.2 billion to the American economy in 2023. GAMA delivered 1,782 piston aircraft and 854 business jets in 2025 alone. Every one of those aircraft needs a roof, and the roofs are not being built as fast as the aircraft are being delivered.

So the decision is not really buy or build. It is whether the owner controls the roof or rents it from someone who does — and whether the lender who finances it understands the difference between a building and an airplane. The owner who builds needs a construction loan that converts to permanent financing, a ground lease the lender will accept, and a rate that does not eat the deal. The owner who buys needs a lender who will value a twenty-year-old steel building on leased land. Both of them need the same thing first: a lender who does hangar loans. There are not many. The name that says so, plainly, is the one they will find.
