What a Hangar Loan Costs in 2026: Rates, Terms, and the Ground-Lease Problem

A hangar loan runs from 2.76 percent on a Georgia public-airport program to 9.75 percent on a typical SBA deal. The rate is the easy part. The ground lease is the part that kills the loan.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The loan file was clean in every way a bank usually cares about. The borrower owned a turboprop worth more than the hangar he wanted to build. His credit score was above 750. His business had three years of tax returns showing steady profit. The hangar was a 60-by-60 pre-engineered steel box at a public airport in Texas, priced at $240,000 all-in. The lender said no anyway — not because of the borrower, but because of the land. The hangar would sit on a ground lease with eleven years left on it, and the airport authority would not commit to a renewal. The building was financeable. The dirt under it was not. That is the hangar loan market in one file: the asset is straightforward, the land is the problem, and the problem is priced into every term a lender offers.

Start with the rates, because they span a wider band than almost any other niche in commercial lending. At the bottom sits public money: Georgia's Transportation Infrastructure Bank, which lends to publicly owned airports for hangar construction, charges 2.76 percent on a five-year loan, 3.04 percent on ten years, 3.58 percent on fifteen, and 4.03 percent on twenty. Those are subsidized rates for public airports, not for private borrowers, and they come with an application fee of $250 and an awards process that runs on the state's calendar. At the other end sits the Small Business Administration. Across 75 SBA 7(a) loans to aviation support businesses approved between October 2023 and June 2026, the median loan was $250,000, the median rate at approval was 9.75 percent — prime plus 2.75 points — and the median term was ten years. Seventy-three percent of those loans were $500,000 or less. Between the two poles sits the specialist commercial lender: Commercial Loans of Texas, a direct lender that has financed hangars since 1998, quotes rates on the file rather than publishing them, with a $200,000 minimum, 15-to-25-year amortization, and a typical close in three to five weeks.

The reason the band is so wide is the ground lease, and it is worth understanding precisely, because it is the term that decides whether a hangar is financeable at any rate. Most hangars at public-use airports sit on land the borrower does not own. The airport authority or municipal sponsor owns the dirt; the borrower owns the improvements — the hangar, the office, the fuel farm — and leases the ground beneath them. Grand County, Colorado, offers ground leases that generally run 25 years with an option to renew for 25 more, at $0.3627 per square foot per year in 2026, rising with the consumer price index. Cox Field in Paris, Texas, starts at $0.25 per square foot on a five-year initial term, adjusted by CPI at renewal. The lease term, the remaining years, and the transferability all become core parts of the collateral analysis. A hangar with 40 years left on its ground lease underwrites very differently from one with eight — and a hangar with 11 years left and no renewal commitment may not underwrite at all.

Loan-to-value is where the ground lease shows up as a number. Commercial Loans of Texas puts typical maximum LTV for hangar and FBO properties at 55 to 70 percent, with the discount applied precisely because the use is specialized and the buyer pool is thin. That compares with 75 to 80 percent for a conventional commercial property. The lender's financeable profile is specific: a long remaining ground lease term, typically 20-plus years, with clear transfer and assignment rights, plus airport sponsor sign-off on the deal. A ground-up hangar construction with no pre-leased tenant and no airport sponsor sign-off is the profile the same lender lists as difficult. The math follows: on a $240,000 hangar at 60 percent LTV, the borrower brings $96,000 in cash and borrows $144,000. On the same building at 75 percent LTV, the cash drops to $60,000. The ground lease costs the borrower $36,000 of extra equity before the first payment is due.

The appraisal problem compounds it. Banks classify hangars as outbuildings — the same category as a detached garage or a barn — and outbuildings get minimal appraised value relative to what they cost to build. A pilot who spends $150,000 to $300,000 building a hangar at an airpark may find the appraiser gives $40,000 of credit for it. The gap is not the appraiser's fault. There are few comparable sales, the buyer pool is small, and the building's value depends entirely on the ground lease surviving. That is why the most common way airpark buyers finance a hangar is not a hangar loan at all: they buy the home with a conventional mortgage and build the hangar with cash, or they buy the home, wait for it to close, and then use a home equity line to fund construction. The third route is the specialist: companies like Air Capital Group and HomePromise focus on aviation real estate, understand taxiway easements, and will not flinch at runway proximity on the appraisal. Some FBO construction companies offer direct financing at 85 percent loan-to-value with five-to-ten-year terms — but the rates run in commercial-loan territory, well above a conventional mortgage.

There is a counterargument, and it comes from the lenders who do this work every day. The ground lease is not a defect in the collateral. It is the collateral, properly understood. An airport is a permanent institution; a 25-year ground lease with a renewal option is a longer horizon than most commercial leases, and the airport authority has every incentive to keep a hangar occupied and rent-paying. The LTV discount is not a penalty for the lease. It is the price of a thin buyer pool, and the buyer pool is thin because so few people own airplanes — not because the asset is bad. A hangar at a busy reliever airport with a 20-year waitlist is a better credit than a strip retail center with a five-year lease and a tenant who can leave. The lenders who understand aviation price that in. The lenders who do not, decline the file and call the asset unfinanceable. Both are right about their own underwriting. Only one of them is right about the hangar.

What a borrower should do is specific and unglamorous. Before spending a dollar on design, get the ground lease in writing: the remaining term, the renewal options, the assignment language, and the airport sponsor's written consent to a transfer on sale or foreclosure. If the remaining term is under 20 years, negotiate the renewal before applying for the loan, because the lender will price the short lease whether the borrower mentions it or not. Bring the specialist lender into the deal early — the bank that has never seen a ground lease will spend a month learning what the specialist already knows, and the construction season will pass while it learns. And size the equity for 60 percent LTV, not 75, because the hangar will appraise for less than it costs to build. The loan exists. It just costs more, in equity and in rate, than the building's price tag suggests — and the extra cost is the ground lease, priced honestly.
