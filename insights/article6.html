How Long It Takes to Build a Hangar — and What the Loan Covers While You Wait

A pre-engineered hangar takes four to eight months from first sketch to first airplane. A custom one takes twelve to twenty-four. The construction loan pays in draws — and the draw schedule is where most builds stall.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The steel arrived in week nine, and sat in the yard for three weeks, because the concrete slab had not cured and the door had not shipped. The owner of a 50-by-60 hangar at a Texas airpark had budgeted four months from contract to completion. He moved his airplane in at month seven. Nothing in the delay was unusual. The design review took 30 to 60 days. The permit took five weeks. The slab needed a month to cure. The bi-fold door — the single most expensive component after the steel itself — arrived six weeks after it was ordered. Every hangar build has the same shape: a long slow front end of paper, a fast middle of steel and concrete, and a tail of doors, electrical, and inspections that stretches longer than anyone budgets. The loan that funds it has to match that shape, or the builder stops work while the money catches up.

The honest range for a private hangar is four to eight months for a pre-engineered steel building, and twelve to twenty-four months for a custom or commercial facility. The difference is not the steel. It is everything around it. Planning and design run three to six months on a complex build. Permitting runs anywhere from a few weeks to several months, depending on the county and the airport authority — and at a public airport, the Federal Aviation Administration gets a say as well: a Form 7460-1 obstruction study can take up to 60 days, so it has to be filed early or construction waits on it. Site preparation and foundation take several weeks to a few months. Construction itself runs three to six months for a pre-fabricated metal building, or nine to eighteen months and more for a large custom structure. Cox Field in Paris, Texas, tells builders to plan six to eight months from start to finish without delays, and its guide is the rare official document that admits the timeline out loud.

The cost tiers explain why the timeline stretches. A bare metal shell — pre-engineered steel, no insulation, no finished floor, basic roll-up door — runs $25 to $35 per square foot for the building kit alone. A 50-by-60 structure, 3,000 square feet, costs roughly $75,000 to $105,000 for the shell, before the slab, the door, electrical, or site prep. The functional hangar most owners actually build adds insulation, a proper bi-fold or hydraulic door, basic electrical, and a six-inch reinforced concrete slab, and lands at $40 to $90 per square foot all in — $120,000 to $270,000 for the same 50-by-60. The door alone accounts for $15,000 to $50,000 of that spread. Add 220-volt service, LED lighting, water, compressed air, an epoxied floor, climate control, and internet, and the build reaches $90 to $150 per square foot, or $270,000 to $450,000. The full custom build — living quarters, office, bathroom, loft, finished workshop — starts at $150 per square foot and keeps going. A standard 15,000-square-foot box hangar costs $900,000 to $1,800,000. Executive hangars for corporate jets run $1 million to $3 million. Maintenance facilities run $2 million to more than $8 million. Post-frame construction, the cheapest path, puts a single-aircraft hangar in Montana at $45,000 to $120,000, with a build of six to twelve weeks from contract to completion.

What the loan covers while the owner waits is the part that surprises first-time builders. A construction loan does not hand over the full amount on day one. It pays in draws, tied to milestones: foundation poured, steel erected, roof on, door installed, certificate of occupancy issued. Each draw requires an inspection, and each inspection takes days. The lender's inspector verifies the work before the money moves, and the builder does not start the next phase until the previous draw clears. That is the correct way to run a construction loan — it is also why a build stalls for three weeks while a door ships. The draws that fund each stage have to be sized to the actual cost of that stage, and the most common error is a draw schedule written from the contractor's estimate rather than from the supplier's lead times. The door is ordered in week two and arrives in week eight. The draw for the door is released when the door is installed. The six weeks in between are paid for by the owner's cash, or by nobody, and the build waits.

The regulatory tail is the part no schedule survives intact. Fire protection must meet NFPA 409, the standard for aircraft hangars, and the fire suppression system — foam, in most cases — is a line item that surprises owners who budgeted for a building and forgot the standard. Electrical systems are designed for hazardous locations, because fuel vapor and sparks do not mix. Structural engineering must accommodate clear spans of up to 300 feet, and the specialized hangar doors cost $15,000 to $30,000 each before installation. Annual upkeep runs $5,000 to $15,000. None of this is hidden. It is all in the builder's quote, on page three, below the number the owner remembers. The builds that finish on time are the ones where the owner read page three.

The counterargument is that the timeline problem is overstated, and there is a version of it that holds up. A pre-engineered steel hangar is one of the fastest commercial buildings to erect: the steel is fabricated off-site, the parts arrive numbered, and a competent crew can have the structure up in weeks. The four-to-eight-month figure is mostly paper — design review, permitting, the FAA study — and paper can be filed in parallel. An owner who submits the FAA form on day one, orders the door in week two, and has the slab curing while the permit is pending can cut two months off the schedule. The builders who quote twelve to twenty-four months are quoting the custom commercial job, not the private hangar, and the two should not be confused. The honest timeline for a standard private hangar, properly managed, is closer to five months than eight.

What the loan should look like follows from that. A construction-to-permanent loan — one closing, one set of fees, converting to a permanent loan at completion — beats two separate closings for most private builds, because the second closing is where the appraisal gap appears: the hangar appraises for less than it cost to build, and the permanent lender prices that gap. The draw schedule should be written around supplier lead times, not construction phases, with the door and the electrical ordered early and the draws released against delivery, not installation. And the contingency should be 15 percent, not 10, because the line that overruns is always the one nobody priced — the apron between the hangar and the taxiway, which at Cox Field the builder pays for at the owner's sole expense, or the electrical service the airport requires but the quote omitted. A hangar built in five months on a loan that pays in the right order is a good project. The same hangar built in seven months on a loan that pays late is the same building, two months later, with the same interest bill.
