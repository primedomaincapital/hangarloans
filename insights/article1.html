The Loan Nobody Wants to Make

A hangar is a six- or seven-figure asset that almost no lender understands. That gap is the whole story of hangar financing.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 8, 2026 · 7 min read

A developer in Camarillo, California, had the land, the tenant, and the drawings. The hangar was a luxury executive facility with 255,000 square feet of ramp space, built for aircraft owners who wanted privacy, security, and their own office above the concrete. What he did not have, when he started, was a lender. The banks he called first understood commercial real estate. They did not understand that the land belonged to an airport, that the building sat on a ground lease, or that the federal government had a say in what happened on that ground. The deal got done — with Signature, a division of Esquire Bank, a bank that keeps an aviation desk — but only after the developer had learned what every hangar borrower learns: the money for the building is harder to find than the money for the airplane inside it.

That is the odd shape of this market. A 15,000-square-foot hangar costs $900,000 to $1,800,000 to build at $60 to $120 per square foot, and larger jet facilities run past $2 million. The aircraft that parks inside it can cost ten times that. Yet the jet is the easy loan and the hangar is the hard one, because the jet is a registered, serial-numbered, repossessable asset with a liquid resale market, and the hangar is a steel building bolted to land the borrower does not own, at an airport whose rules the lender has never read.

AOPA Finance states the first half of the problem plainly. Its lenders use the aircraft as collateral for the loan, and they are unable to include financing for the purchase of a hangar. In rare circumstances, AOPA says, a lender has lent against an airplane for the purchase of a hangar — but if the owner buys both at the same time, there is not enough equity in the airplane to cover both. Its advice is to check with a local lender that has commercial real estate experience. That is honest advice, and it is also a description of a market with no front door: the aviation lenders do not do buildings, and the building lenders do not do aviation.

The second half of the problem is the ground. A hangar at a public airport usually sits on leased land, and the lease carries federal obligations — grant assurances, non-discrimination rules, reversion clauses — that a conventional commercial lender reads as risk it cannot price. The few lenders who do this work have learned the paperwork the way a pilot learns a checklist: Signature's aviation banking page lists hangar construction and permanent financing, FBO improvement and working capital, and loans on leased land as separate products, because each one answers a different question about who owns what when the loan ends. Nick Martino, vice president of operations for CloudNine, the private hangar complex in Camarillo, put the point in a testimonial on that same page: "One of the key challenges we face in aviation is that it's very difficult to find a lender with an in-depth understanding of our industry. That is what makes Signature so unique — I don't have to explain aviation industry nuances, best practices or federal obligations. They know our space."

Where the private market will not go, the states have stepped in, and their programs show how thin the private market is. Nebraska's Revolving Hangar Loan Program, run by the state Department of Transportation's Division of Aeronautics, lends up to 80% of eligible costs, to a maximum of $1,000,000 per project, with a 20-year repayment period and no interest at all. The revolving fund was established in 1992, and the program rules were re-approved by the Nebraska Aeronautics Commission on May 8, 2026. California's Local Airport Loan Program makes state loans to eligible public airports for hangars, general-aviation terminals, and fueling facilities, with a maximum term of 17 years and rates set at the latest California General Obligation Bond sale rate. A state that lends interest-free for twenty years is not competing with private lenders. It is filling a hole they left.

The pattern repeats at the municipal level. In Fayetteville, Arkansas, in June 2006, the city council approved two $250,000 state grants and a $700,000 city loan, amortized over fifteen years at 5%, to build two corporate hangars at Drake Field — because every hangar at the airport was full, there was a waiting list, and private investors were unwilling to build a building on property they did not own. The staff memo noted the new hangars would generate about $7,600 a month. Twenty years later the same sentence could be written about a hundred American airports: the hangars are full, the waiting list is real, and the investors are still unwilling to build on land they do not own.

The counterargument is that the market is working, just slowly. AOPA Finance offers terms of 5 to 20 years with a typical down payment of 15%, and its published rates for experimental aircraft run 4.99% to 6.9%, with piston-aircraft rates at 6.99% to 9.99% as of November 2026 — down from the peak of the last two years. Specialist lenders exist, state programs exist, and deals do close. The CloudNine hangar in Camarillo closed. The Fayetteville hangars were built. But a market where the borrower must assemble the financing from an aircraft broker, a state revolving fund, and a city council resolution is not a deep market. It is a market of exceptions, and exceptions are expensive: every one of them costs the borrower time, and time is what a developer with a signed tenant and a construction window does not have.

That is why the name matters. A lender who does hangar loans needs to be found by the borrower who has just been told no by two banks and an aircraft broker — and the borrower searches for the words "hangar loans." A developer who needs construction financing and then permanent financing needs one counterparty who understands both draws, and that counterparty needs a name that says so before the first call. The scarcity of lenders is not a problem for the name. It is the reason the name has value: in a market with few sellers, the seller who is easiest to find wins the deal.
