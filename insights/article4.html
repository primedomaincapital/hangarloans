The Hangar Is Now an Asset Class

One company has raised $318 million in equity and borrowed $150 million in tax-exempt bonds to build private hangar campuses. The roof over a jet is becoming what the jet never was: an institutionally owned building.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

In the second quarter of 2026, a company most Americans have never heard of reported something it had never reported before: positive operating cash flow. The company was Sky Harbour Group, listed on the New York Stock Exchange as SKYH, and its business is building private hangar campuses at American airports. Revenue was about $9.86 million, up roughly 50% from a year earlier. The number was small. The fact that it existed at all was the story — because for the first time, a hangar was paying for itself the way an office building does.

Sky Harbour was founded in 2017 by Tal Keinan with a simple observation: the country was short of hangars, and the shortage was worst exactly where the jets were going. The company secures long-term ground leases at airports serving business aviation, builds a standardized hangar campus, and leases the finished facilities to aircraft owners, corporate flight departments, charter operators, and government clients, on terms of one to ten years. From breaking ground to opening takes about 14 months. It now holds 22 ground leases across the United States, with a pipeline targeting roughly 200 airports and a goal of adding four to six new leases a year. At the end of 2025 it operated eight campuses — Sugar Land, Nashville, Miami-Opa Locka, San Jose, Camarillo, Phoenix Deer Valley, Dallas Addison, and Denver Centennial — with about 1.03 million square feet of operating hangar, office, and support space, 85 tenant leases, no tenant above 10% of revenue, and a 5.6-year weighted-average lease term. Future minimum noncancelable tenant lease payments contracted at the end of 2025 totaled $70.689 million, with $21.625 million due in 2026.

The economics are what make the model worth watching. Tenants sign triple-net leases: base rent plus property taxes, insurance, and maintenance, which means the tenant, not the landlord, absorbs most operating expenses. At stabilized campuses the operating margin already runs around 89%. Re-leasing over the past year pulled in roughly 19% higher rents on renewal — one research update counted 119,000 square feet re-leased over twelve months at a 23% revenue step-up — on top of built-in CPI escalators with a 4% floor. Rents vary sharply by market: about $80 a square foot at the San Jose campus, in the low $20s at some Texas locations. Nashville runs at 102% occupancy and generates $5.5 million in annualized revenue; Sugar Land sits at 100%. The Opa Locka Phase 2 campus, which opened in May, was already at 80% combined occupancy with a $10.1 million revenue run rate. San Jose Phase 1 runs at 132% economic occupancy — above 100% because the company sub-leases shared hangar space to more than one tenant.

The capital behind the campuses is the other half of the story, and it is the half that matters most to anyone who finances hangars. Sky Harbour has raised $318 million in equity since 2021 to build its portfolio. It closed a $150 million Series 2026 tax-exempt bond financing at 6.00% in early 2026, and a second bond deal followed. In August 2026 it raised another $40 million in a registered direct equity placement at $10 a share, and it expects about $94 million more from public warrant exercises scheduled for January 2027. At the end of the first quarter of 2026 it held $187.6 million in cash, restricted cash, and investments, with about $368.2 million in total available resources including a JPMorgan warehouse facility. Assets under construction and completed construction topped $393 million at the end of the second quarter. The chief financial officer, Francisco Gonzalez, came to the role after nearly three decades in investment banking at Goldman Sachs and RBC, where he advised on airport infrastructure bond deals. A hangar developer whose CFO spent his career structuring airport bonds is not a coincidence. It is the business model.

The demand underneath it is structural, not cyclical. The company's 2025 annual report notes that the physical footprint of the U.S. business-aviation fleet expanded by almost 46 million square feet over sixteen years, with cumulative fleet area up 73% from 2010 through 2025. Modern business aircraft are getting larger, which renders much of the existing hangar stock functionally obsolete — a jet built for a 1990s T-hangar does not fit in one. McKinsey's first State of Business Aviation report counted a record 3.9 million departures in 2025, growing 4.9% a year since 2019, with flight hours up 37% over the same period. NetJets president Patrick Gallagher told McKinsey that infrastructure "is by far the biggest challenge we contend with." The jets keep coming. The roofs do not.

The counterargument is that the story is still mostly promise. The second quarter's net loss was $4.37 million, and adjusted EBITDA was still negative $947,000. The first positive quarter of consolidated operating cash flow was about $500,000 — an inflection point, not an income engine. The company guides to an annualized adjusted EBITDA run rate of $4 million to $6 million by year-end 2026; against a $393 million asset base, $6 million is a yield of roughly 1.5% before debt service, corporate overhead, or share count. Lease-up is uneven: Denver Centennial sits at 44% occupancy, which management acknowledged on the earnings call as a disappointment, in a market where competition from existing FBOs is entrenched. Free cash flow is not expected to turn meaningfully positive until 2028. And the capital intensity has a cost in dilution: the August raise took the share count from 34.5 million to 38.5 million, and the January 2027 warrant exercises could push it toward 48 million. Four to six new leases a year is the target — and some leases in the current pipeline have been in negotiation for five years.

What matters for hangar finance is not whether Sky Harbour pays a dividend in 2027. It is that the money for hangars has moved. A decade ago, a hangar was financed, if at all, by a local bank that understood commercial real estate and had to be taught the ground lease. Now it is financed by tax-exempt bonds, warehouse facilities, and public equity — the same capital markets that fund offices and warehouses. The developer who needs a hangar built negotiates with an institutional landlord, not a loan officer, and the landlord's terms are triple-net leases with CPI escalators and a 4% floor. That leaves the other side of the market exactly where it was: the owner who wants his own building, on his own ground lease, with a construction loan that converts to permanent financing. The institutional money has arrived for the landlord. It has not arrived for the owner. He still needs a lender who does hangar loans — and he still searches for the words that say so.
